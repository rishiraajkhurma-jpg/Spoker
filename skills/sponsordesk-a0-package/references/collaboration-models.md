# Collaboration models: every way a company and an event can work together

Money is one way to close a deal, not the only one. Many good partnerships have no cash at all: the company brings a product, a service or an opportunity, the attendees benefit directly, the organizers save cost or raise the event's quality, and the company gets visibility, users or hires. SponsorDesk treats each of these as a real deal with the same pipeline, MOU and delivery tracking as a cash deal.

Every model below is judged from three points of view, because a deal only lasts when all three win:

- **Organizers (E-Cell / fest team):** cash raised, cost avoided, effort to run it, prestige, risk.
- **The event and its audience (attendees, startups, speakers):** does this make the event better for the people who come? Real value, or an ad they will ignore?
- **The company (marketing, HR, partnerships, product, or a dealer/distributor team):** what outcome can they report upward: users, leads, hires, sales, brand lift, CSR spend, content?

## The triple-win test (apply to every proposed deal)

| Question | Must be yes |
|---|---|
| Organizers: does this either bring cash, or replace a cost we would otherwise pay, or raise the event's standing? | yes |
| Audience: would an attendee be glad this was there, not just tolerate it? | yes |
| Company: is there a number they can measure and report after the event? | yes |
| Fit: does it avoid clashing with an existing partner's exclusivity or the institute's rules? | yes |

If any answer is no, redesign the deal (change the activation, add a measurable element, or pick a different model) before pitching it.

## A. Cash models (company pays)

### A1. Tiered sponsorship (Title / Platinum / Gold / Silver)
- Company gets: logo placement, booth, mentions, content, scaled by tier.
- Organizers get: predictable cash, simple to sell.
- Audience gets: little directly, unless paired with an activation.
- Use when: brand wants broad visibility, procurement prefers a standard package.
- Watch: logo-only deals renew poorly. Always attach at least one activation.

### A2. Property naming
"<Brand> Pitch Prize", "<Brand> Hackathon", "<Brand> Main Stage", "<Brand> Startup Expo".
- Company: ownership of one thing people remember, repeated in every mention.
- Organizers: high price per unit of effort.
- Audience: neutral to positive, especially when the brand funds the prize.
- Valuation: price by how often the property is named (posts, stage, certificates).

### A3. Paid brand integration (content partnership)
The brand is built into the event's content, not just placed next to it:
- Co-created reels and posts (brand's product used naturally in the content)
- "Presented by" segments: a speaker series, a podcast episode recorded at the event, a live show
- Product placement on stage (laptops used in the hackathon, the payment app used for the food court)
- Branded challenges inside sessions (quiz rounds, audience polls)
- Company: native reach, content they can reuse on their own channels (agree usage rights).
- Organizers: cash plus better production.
- Audience: good if it is entertaining or useful, bad if it is a forced ad.
- Rule: the brand approves the content, the organizers keep editorial control, and paid content is labelled as a partnership where platforms require it (Instagram/LinkedIn paid-partnership tag; ASCI influencer guidelines in India).

### A4. Experience / activation zone
The brand runs a game, demo, photo booth, test drive, VR station, coding challenge or lounge.
- Company: direct interaction, opt-in leads, product trials.
- Organizers: fee plus a livelier venue.
- Audience: fun or useful thing to do between sessions.
- Measure: visitors (counted), sign-ups, trials, codes redeemed.

### A5. Hiring / talent partnership
Internship fair table, pre-placement talks, a hiring challenge, fast-track interviews, opt-in resume book.
- Company: hires and employer brand among a hard-to-reach audience.
- Organizers: fee from HR budgets (often separate from marketing budgets, so a second door into the same company).
- Audience: real jobs and internships. Strong attendee value.
- Rule: resume data only with each student's explicit opt-in.

### A6. Problem statement / case competition partner
The company sets a real business problem; teams compete; winners present to company leaders.
- Company: ideas, a talent pipeline, and engaged hours with the brand (far more than a logo).
- Organizers: fee plus a strong flagship event.
- Audience: real-world problem, prize, chance of a job or internship.
- Watch: agree IP ownership of solutions upfront (default: participants keep IP; company gets a licence to evaluate, or buys rights from winners).

### A7. Workshop / masterclass sponsor
The company's experts teach a skill using their product (cloud, design tools, trading platform, AI tools).
- Company: product adoption, developer or user sign-ups.
- Organizers: fee or free expert content.
- Audience: a skill and often free credits.

### A8. Performance-linked (base + bonus)
A smaller fixed fee plus a bonus per measurable result: per verified sign-up, app install, application, or sale through a unique code or link.
- Company: pays partly for results, which is easier to approve internally.
- Organizers: lower guaranteed cash, upside if the audience converts.
- Rule: tracking must be the company's own system (their codes/dashboard) with a reporting date in the MOU. Set a cap and a minimum.

### A9. Annual / multi-event partnership
One deal across the organizers' full year (all E-Cell events, newsletters, competitions), not just one weekend.
- Company: sustained presence, better price per touchpoint.
- Organizers: bigger, steadier cash, one negotiation instead of five.
- Audience: consistent benefits through the year.

### A10. CSR partnership
Funding a social-impact track, rural entrepreneurship programme, women founders cohort, or education initiative.
- Company: spend that counts toward CSR obligations (Companies Act 2013, Section 135 / Schedule VII) when structured correctly.
- Organizers: access to a budget that marketing teams do not control.
- Audience: impact-led programming.
- Rule: CSR money usually needs an eligible implementing agency and specific paperwork; involve the institute's office before promising it qualifies.

## B. No-money models (products, services, opportunities)

### B1. Product partner
Food and beverages, snacks, energy drinks, merch, stationery, gadgets, gift hampers, prizes.
- Company: sampling to thousands of people, product in hand, photos and content.
- Organizers: replaces a cost (food, prizes, kits) they would otherwise pay.
- Audience: free stuff that is actually useful.
- Valuation: count at what the organizers would have paid (cost avoided), not the brand's MRP.

### B2. Services partner
Cloud credits, SaaS licences, design tools, AI API credits, legal or CA office hours for startups, banking/startup accounts, insurance, logistics.
- Company: activated users who may convert to paying customers later.
- Organizers: replaces tools or services the event or its startups need.
- Audience: startups and students get real resources (often worth more than cash prizes).
- Measure: accounts activated, credits redeemed.

### B3. Opportunity partner (audience benefits directly)
The company offers something money cannot easily buy:
- Internships or fast-track interviews for winners
- Incubation / accelerator interviews, investor meetings, demo-day slots
- Mentorship hours with company leaders
- Factory, office or lab visits
- Pilot projects or proof-of-concept deals for startups
- Scholarships, certification vouchers, course access
- Company: talent, deal flow, goodwill, a story to tell.
- Organizers: raises the event's value proposition without spending.
- Audience: the strongest attendee benefit of any model.
- Rule: write the opportunity precisely in the MOU (how many, eligibility, by when) so it is actually delivered.

### B4. Venue, travel and logistics partner
Cab or mobility partner (ride credits), hotel partner (speaker stays), printing partner, AV/stage partner, internet partner, courier partner.
- Company: brand presence plus usage.
- Organizers: big cost savings on hard line items.
- Audience: smoother event (rides, wifi).

### B5. Media partner
News outlets, newsletters, podcasts, YouTube channels, startup media.
- Company (the media house): content, access to speakers, audience growth.
- Organizers: coverage and reach.
- Audience: coverage of their startups.
- Exchange: logo and "media partner" title in return for agreed articles, posts or coverage.

### B6. Knowledge partner
Consulting firms, research firms, industry bodies: a report launched at the event, a session, judges.
- Company: thought leadership.
- Organizers: credibility and content.
- Audience: insight and expert access.

### B7. Platform / tech partner
Ticketing, payments, registration, event app, livestream, CRM, AI tools used to run the event.
- Company: thousands of users onboarded onto their platform, a case study.
- Organizers: tools for free.
- Audience: smoother registration and payments.

### B8. Offer / discount partner
Exclusive codes for attendees (food delivery, subscriptions, travel, courses, gadgets).
- Company: customer acquisition at a known cost.
- Organizers: an attendee benefit to advertise.
- Audience: real savings.
- Measure: redemptions on the company's dashboard.

### B9. Community / cross-promotion partner
Other fests, clubs, student communities, startup communities, co-working spaces.
- Company/community: reach into our audience in exchange for theirs.
- Organizers: registrations and reach.
- Rule: track with separate registration links so the value is visible.

### B10. Volunteer / expertise partner
A company lends people: judges, mentors, photographers, developers for the event app.
- Company: employee engagement, visibility for their people.
- Organizers: skilled help.

## C. Hybrid models

### C1. Cash + in-kind
The most common real deal: e.g. INR 3 lakh cash plus food for 2,000 people. Counts toward a tier at the agreed value.

### C2. Revenue share
The company sells at the event (food stalls, merch, books, course enrolments, ticket bundles) and shares a percentage of sales, sometimes plus a small fixed fee.
- Organizers: income with no upfront negotiation over budget.
- Watch: needs trusted sales reporting; set a minimum guarantee.

### C3. Sampling partner with a fee
Small fee plus product: company gets exclusive sampling rights in an area.

### C4. Dealer / distributor co-op
For brands sold through dealers (phones, laptops, two-wheelers, consumer goods): the local dealer or distributor runs the stall and sells or books test rides, while the brand's co-op marketing fund pays part of the fee.
- Company: the brand gets visibility; the dealer gets sales and leads.
- Organizers: two budgets to tap (brand + dealer).
- Rule: the MOU must name who is responsible for what (brand vs dealer).

### C5. Prize pool partner
The company funds or provides the prizes (cash, products, credits, opportunities) for a competition in return for naming and judging seats.

## Valuing non-cash deals (so they fit the tier ladder)

1. **Cost-avoided value**: what the organizers would otherwise have paid in the market for this item (quotes or last year's bills). This is the number that counts toward a tier.
2. **Attendee value**: what an attendee would pay for it (credits, courses). Use this in marketing ("INR 50 lakh in credits for startups"), never as the tier value.
3. **Retail/MRP**: the company's own figure. Note it, do not use it for tiers.
4. Tier credit = cost-avoided value, unless the manager approves a multiplier for strategically important items (e.g. internships for winners).
5. Cap: in-kind may cover up to 50% of a tier's value without manager approval; above that is an exception.
6. Anything the organizers cannot use (excess product, credits nobody redeems) has zero value. Agree quantities based on real need.

## Choosing the model for a company (A1 and A5 use this)

| Company's main goal | Best models |
|---|---|
| Hiring / employer brand | A5, A6, B3 (internships), A7 |
| Users / sign-ups / app installs | A4, A7, A8, B2, B8, B7 |
| Brand awareness | A1, A2, A3, C3, B1 |
| Sales (offline products) | C2, C4, B1 with C3 |
| Startup ecosystem / deal flow | B3, B2, A6, C5 |
| Thought leadership | B6, A7, A3 |
| CSR spend | A10 |
| Content for their own channels | A3, A6 |
| Low or no marketing budget this quarter | Move from cash to B1, B2, B3, B8 rather than losing the company |

A company that says "no budget" is moved down this table to a no-money model, not marked LOST.

## What changes in the pipeline for non-cash deals

- `deals.deal_type`: `cash | in_kind | opportunity | hybrid | revenue_share | performance`.
- A6 issues an MOU with a "What each side provides" table for both sides. For pure in-kind deals there is no payment invoice; instead A6 tracks **delivery of the company's side** (stage `INKIND_CONFIRMED` once products/services/opportunities are received or confirmed in writing). Tax treatment of barter must be checked with the institute's accounts office or CA.
- A7 tracks delivery in both directions: what we give them and what they give the audience (e.g. internships actually offered, credits actually issued). The results report shows both.
